\large Volatility Forecasts, the Volmex \BVIV{} Index and What 15-Minute VWAP Timing Is Worth}
\author{Gregor Albiez}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Bitcoin implied volatility has become directly tradable: perpetual futures on the Volmex \BVIV{} index have listed on Bitfinex since 2024 and on Hyperliquid since September 2026. We ask how BTC volatility should be forecast when a 30-day implied index is observable, and whether a long-\BVIV{} position timed by a VWAP signal on 15-minute bars hedges spot or perpetual BTC exposure better than a minimum-variance hedge. In a calibrated market where the \BVIV{} perpetual is priced without arbitrage, we evaluate seven forecasting models and \NRules{} hedging rules with strict separation between training and \NTest{} test paths. Implied volatility is the best forecast at 30 days, HAR-type models at one day, and HAR augmented with implied variance is robust across horizons. A minimum-variance \BVIV{} hedge cuts expected shortfall by \EsRedAlways\% for \CostAlways\% of notional a year; volatility hedges stay partial because BTC's spot-vol correlation is weak and changes sign across regimes. Switching the hedge on and off around VWAP destroys value through turnover. A VWAP \emph{ratchet}, which keeps a minimum-variance core and adds protection on breakdowns, lifts the ES reduction to \EsRedRatchet\%. A timing placebo shows what drives that gain. VWAP breakdowns do carry information: rules that hedge only after a breakdown beat randomly timed copies by up to \PgridMaxAbs{} percentage points. But such rules protect less and cost more than a core hedge, and once the core is held, the timing value vanishes and the gain is a size effect. The placebo is the yardstick for any intraday hedge signal, and our pipeline applies it unchanged to live Binance, Bitfinex and Volmex data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A holder of bitcoin, whether in spot or through a perpetual swap, is exposed to two kinds of risk: an ordinary random walk, and sudden crashes in which liquidity disappears and implied volatility spikes. Delta hedges with futures remove the first and, with it, the position itself. Crash protection has historically required options. A cheaper and more modular instrument now exists. The Volmex \BVIV{} index measures 30-day BTC implied volatility in a model-free way \citep{volmex2024iv}. It has traded as a USDT-margined perpetual on Bitfinex since April 2024 \citep{coindesk2024bitfinex}, on gTrade since June 2025 \citep{coindesk2025gtrade}, and as \texttt{mkts:BVIV} on Hyperliquid since 21 September 2026 \citep{coindesk2026hyperliquid}. Regulated alternatives followed: CME announced Bitcoin Volatility futures in May 2026 \citep{cme2026bvi}.

Whether a long-volatility position hedges a long-BTC book depends on the spot-vol correlation. In BTC this correlation is weak and unstable. Positive return shocks have historically raised volatility more than negative ones \citep{bouri2017return,baur2018asymmetric}, an asymmetry that has faded over time \citep{takaishi2021time}. The correlation between BTC and DVOL turned strongly positive in early 2023 \citep{coindesk2023dvol}, while \BVIV{} trended down through the 2024--25 rally \citep{coindesk2025rally}. Equity investors learned that long-volatility hedges look good \emph{ex ante} but are eroded by carry and costs \citep{whaley2013trading,alexander2016diversification,israelov2019pathetic}, except in genuine crises \citep{szado2009vix}. This raises the question of whether a hedge can be \emph{timed}. This paper tests a timing signal that every intraday trader already watches, price relative to its volume-weighted average, and asks whether it carries information about crash risk beyond simply holding more hedge.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (i) We compare seven daily volatility forecasts, including the \BVIV{}-implied forecast, with robust loss functions. (ii) We derive the P\&L of a BTC book hedged with \BVIV{} perpetuals, splitting funding into an arbitrage-free \emph{drift transfer} and a \emph{carry premium}. (iii) We design a VWAP ratchet, a hysteresis rule that is fast to protect and slow to un-hedge, show why naive VWAP switching fails, and introduce a \emph{timing placebo} that separates timing information from hedge size. (iv) We report Monte Carlo evidence under a strict train/test protocol and ship a pipeline that reproduces every number on live data.

\section{The BVIV index and the economics of a volatility hedge}\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Index and instruments.} \BVIV{} is a model-free, 30-day constant-maturity implied-volatility index in the variance-swap tradition \citep{volmex2024iv,demeterfi1999guide}. Secondary sources list Deribit and OKX options as inputs. It updates about once per second and is served at 1- to 60-minute resolution by the Volmex API. Deribit's DVOL uses the same VIX-style construction on Deribit options alone \citep{deribit2021dvol}, which makes it a natural substitute for history before 2024. The perpetuals pay one dollar per index point per contract. On Bitfinex the mark price is the index; funding follows the spread between perpetual and mark and can briefly pause trading. On Hyperliquid, funding adds a default interest leg of 0.01\% per eight hours to a premium term \citep{hyperliquid2026funding}. That is about 11\% a year of notional, or roughly \HedgeCarry{} vol points a year at \BVIV{} $\approx$ 55. This leg is our baseline \emph{carry} of a long-volatility position; builder-deployed markets can reconfigure it, so we vary it in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{Variance risk premium.} Implied variance exceeds subsequent realised variance on average. For BTC the premium is about 0.14 a year in variance units, against about 0.02 for the S\&P 500 \citep{almeida2024risk}, which is consistent with \citet{alexander2021bitcoin} and the equity evidence of \citet{carr2009variance}. A hedge that is long \BVIV{} pays this premium indirectly. The index itself has no drift, but the perpetual's funding prices in the index's expected path.

\paragraph{Spot-vol dynamics.} The minimum-variance hedge ratio \citep{johnson1960theory,ederington1979hedging} of BTC against \BVIV{} is proportional to their correlation. Because that correlation is regime-dependent \citep{huang2022leverage}, static ratios are misspecified, and dynamic estimates \citep{kroner1993timevarying,engle2002dcc} are the baseline.

\section{Forecasting BTC volatility}\label{sec:forecast}

\paragraph{Measurement.} From 15-minute log returns $r_{d,i}$ (96 per UTC day) we compute realised variance $\RV_d=\sum_i r_{d,i}^2$, together with bipower variation and semivariances \citep{andersen2003modeling,barndorff2004power,patton2015good}. Fifteen minutes is coarser than the 5-minute rule of thumb \citep{liu2015does}, but it matches the trading frequency of the hedge and is robust to the microstructure of a 24/7 market. We use close-to-close returns and not bar VWAPs, because returns between interval averages are smoothed by about one third \citep{working1960note}.

\paragraph{Models.} Each model forecasts the average daily variance over days $t{+}1,\dots,t{+}h$, $h\in\{1,7,30\}$, using information up to the close of day $t$. The models are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a random walk (RW);
  \item RiskMetrics EWMA \citep{jpmorgan1996riskmetrics};
  \item GARCH(1,1) with Student-$t$ errors, re-estimated monthly;
  \item the HAR model of \citet{corsi2009simple} with a 7-day week;
  \item \BVIV{} itself, both raw ($\IV_t^2/365$, ``IV-raw'') and bias-corrected by OLS (``IV'');
  \item HAR augmented with implied variance (HAR-IV):
\end{itemize}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:har}
\overline{\RV}_{t+1:t+h}=\beta_0+\beta_d\RV_t+\beta_w\overline{\RV}_{t-6:t}+\beta_m\overline{\RV}_{t-29:t}+\beta_{iv}\,\IV_t^2/365+\varepsilon_{t+h}.
\end{equation}
The regressions are re-estimated daily on an expanding window that contains only targets already observed, with no overlap leakage. We evaluate forecasts with QLIKE, $L(\RV,F)=\RV/F-\ln(\RV/F)-1$, which is robust to proxy noise \citep{patton2011volatility}. We also report the Mincer--Zarnowitz $R^2$ and Diebold--Mariano tests against HAR with Newey--West variance \citep{mincer1969evaluation,diebold1995comparing}.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:forecast} and Figure~\ref{fig:forecast} confirm the pattern documented on Deribit data by \citet{hoang2020forecasting}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item At one day, the HAR family is best. HAR-IV is marginally ahead of HAR (median loss ratio \QlHarIvOne) and beats it significantly on only \DmWinHarIvOne\% of paths. Implied volatility alone is clearly worse (\QlIvOne), because a 30-day index cannot track tomorrow's variance, and GARCH on daily returns lags (\QlGarchOne), as \citet{bergsli2022forecasting} found for BTC.
  \item At seven days, the bias-corrected index and HAR-IV tie (\QlIvSeven{} and \QlHarIvSeven{} of HAR's loss).
  \item At 30 days, the index is best (\QlIvThirty) and beats HAR significantly on \DmWinIvThirty\% of paths, never losing (\DmLossIvThirty\%).
\end{itemize}
HAR-IV is never far from the best (\QlHarIvOne, \QlHarIvSeven, \QlHarIvThirty), so we use it as the 30-day forecast for hedging. Raw IV is biased upward by the variance risk premium, and the OLS bias correction removes most of that cost (\QlIvrawThirty{} versus \QlIvThirty{} at 30 days).

\section{A BTC book hedged with BVIV perpetuals}\label{sec:hedge}

\paragraph{P\&L.} The book is long $q$ BTC at price $S_t$ and long $H_t\ge 0$ \BVIV{} perpetual contracts at mark $F_t$, each paying \$1 per index point. A position chosen at the close of bar $t$ earns over bar $t{+}1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pnl}
\Pi_{t+1}=q\,(S_{t+1}-S_t)\;-\;\mathbb{1}_{\text{perp}}\,q\,S_t f_t\Delta\;+\;H_t\,(F_{t+1}-F_t)\;-\;H_t\,\phi_t\;-\;c\,|H_{t+1}-H_t|\,F_{t+1},
\end{equation}
where $f_t$ is the BTC perpetual's funding rate, $\phi_t$ the \BVIV{} funding per contract and $c$ the proportional trading cost. A perpetual with funding intensity $k$ trades at $\E_t[\int_0^\infty k e^{-ks} I_{t+s}\,ds]$, the expected index sampled at a random time \citep{he2022perpetual,ackerer2025perpetual}. In the fast-funding limit the mark equals the index, and funding splits into two parts:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:funding}
\phi_t=\underbrace{\E_t[I_{t+1}]-I_t}_{\text{drift transfer}}+\underbrace{\bar\kappa\,\Delta}_{\text{carry premium}}.
\end{equation}
The first term compensates longs exactly for the index's expected path: after a spike, longs \emph{receive} funding while the index mean-reverts. Only the premium $\bar\kappa$ (vol points a year) is a cost, so we report hedge costs as carry premium plus trading costs, not as raw funding.

\paragraph{Hedge ratios.} The minimum-variance hedge is $H_t^\ast=-qS_t\,\beta_t$, where $\beta_t=\mathrm{Cov}_t(r,\Delta F)/\mathrm{Var}_t(\Delta F)$ is estimated on 15-minute changes with an EWMA (half-life 14 days). We impose $H\ge0$, since a protective hedge is never short volatility. For crash sizing we also estimate a \emph{downside hedge ratio} $\beta^-_t=\E_t[r\,\Delta F\mid r<0]/\E_t[\Delta F^2\mid r<0]$ on falling bars only, shrunk towards $\beta_t$ by its effective sample size, which gives $H^-_t=-qS_t\beta^-_t$. The moments are deliberately not demeaned, so $\beta^-$ minimises the downside second moment of the hedged book and also offsets the average loss on falling bars.

\paragraph{The VWAP signal.} BTC trades 24/7, so we anchor VWAP on a rolling 24-hour window, which avoids an arbitrary session open; Section~\ref{sec:results} also tests a UTC-session anchor. Exchange klines give each bar's exact VWAP as quote volume divided by base volume. For Brownian prices, the gap between the last price and its average over a window $w$ has standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{w/3}$, so the scale-free breakdown statistic is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:z}
z_t=\frac{\ln\!\big(S_t/\mathrm{VWAP}^{24h}_t\big)}{\hat\sigma_t\sqrt{1/3}},
\end{equation}
where $\hat\sigma_t$ is a causal one-day volatility forecast. It is an EWMA of 15-minute squared returns, de-seasonalised by a trailing hour-of-week profile; intraday volatility in crypto follows strong and growing periodic patterns \citep{andersen1997intraday,hansen2024periodicity,eross2019intraday}. In this way the volatility forecast of Section~\ref{sec:forecast} sets the band width. Intraday momentum and reversal both exist in BTC \citep{shen2022bitcoin,wen2022intraday}, so we treat $z$ as a \emph{state} variable, not a return forecast.

\paragraph{Rules.} We compare four families, each with long-only positions, a hedge cap of 50\% of BTC notional and a rebalancing band $b=25\%$. The band follows the no-trade-region logic of \citet{whalley1997asymptotic}: the hedge is only traded when its target moves more than $b$ away from the current position.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Always-on:} $H_t=\lambda H_t^\ast$, scaled by $\lambda\in\{0.5,\dots,3\}$.
  \item \textbf{VWAP-switch:} $H_t=g_tH^-_t$. The gate $g_t$ turns on when $z_t<z_{in}$ and off when $z_t>z_{out}$, after a minimum hold of four hours.
  \item \textbf{VWAP-ratchet:} an overlay $o_t$ jumps to 1 on a breakdown and decays with half-life $\tau$:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:ratchet}
  o_t=\begin{cases}1 & z_t<z_{in}\\ 2^{-1/\tau}\,o_{t-1} & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad
  H_t=\varphi H_t^\ast+o_t\max\!\big(H^-_t\,m_t-\varphi H_t^\ast,\,0\big),
  \end{equation}
  where $\varphi$ is a core floor and $m_t$ an optional size multiplier that grows with forecast ``cheapness'', the log ratio of the HAR-IV variance forecast to implied variance.
  \item \textbf{Oracle:} $H^-_t$ held only during the latent stress regime. It is infeasible and serves as an upper bound for regime timing.
\end{itemize}
The ratchet's asymmetry is the point of the design. Re-triggering inside an episode refreshes $o_t$ without trading, so whipsaw around VWAP costs nothing, whereas the switch pays a round trip every time price crosses the band.

\section{Experimental design}\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Calibrated market.} Live exchange data could not be accessed from the build environment, so we evaluate the rules in a simulated market calibrated to the stylised facts above. The pipeline nevertheless ships loaders for live data (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Variance and returns.} Log-variance is the sum of a slow and a fast Ornstein--Uhlenbeck factor. The fast factor tracks a three-state hidden regime (calm, stress, euphoria; expected durations 30/5/10 days) and receives EGARCH-type shocks \citep{nelson1991conditional}, $u_t=\theta_{R}z_t+\gamma(|z_t|-\sqrt{2/\pi})+\sqrt{1-\theta_R^2-\gamma^2(1-2/\pi)}\,\varepsilon_t$. Here $z_t$ is the return shock. The signed loading $\theta_R$ is negative in calm and stress and positive in euphoria (inverse leverage), and the size loading $\gamma$ makes large moves of either sign raise volatility.
  \item \emph{Jumps and seasonality.} Compound-Poisson jumps cluster in stress and push variance up. Volatility and volume follow hour-of-day and weekend seasonality, and bars are built from one-minute paths so that OHLC and the bar VWAP behave like exchange klines.
  \item \emph{Index.} \BVIV{} equals the model's own expectation of 30-day average variance, inflated by a variance premium and perturbed by a persistent sentiment factor and fixing noise. The expectation is exact over regime paths: a Feynman--Kac product of matrix exponentials of $Q+\mathrm{diag}(V)$ integrates the fast factor's regime-driven drift and jump kicks, and the jump variance of returns is mixed with $e^{Qs}$. A unit test checks it against brute-force simulation.
  \item \emph{Funding.} The drift transfer in \eqref{eq:funding} is computed exactly one bar ahead: summed over regime transitions, by Gauss--Hermite quadrature over jumps, and to second order in Gaussian shocks. A unit test verifies it against brute-force simulation, so the perpetual offers no free lunch from mean reversion.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Protocol.} All signals are causal; unit tests verify that truncating the sample leaves every past value unchanged. Rule parameters are selected on \NTrain{} \emph{training} paths, separate from the \NTest{} \emph{test} paths reported below. This avoids the data-snooping trap of picking the best of many rules on the evaluation sample \citep{white2000reality,hansen2005spa,bailey2014pseudo}. The grid contains \NRules{} rules: $z_{in}\in\{-1,-1.5,-2\}$, $\tau\in\{0.5,1,2,4\}$ days, $\varphi\in\{0,0.5,1\}$, with and without forecast sizing, plus the switch, always-on and oracle families. The selection criterion is the median reduction in expected shortfall (ES$_{97.5}$) of daily returns, measured \emph{net} of carry and trading costs. Hedging is evaluated from day 270, once every forecast and signal exists, and forecasts from day 365. Costs are \Fee{} bp fee plus \Slip{} bp slippage per side on traded notional, and the carry is \HedgeCarry{} vol points a year.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Selected rules.} On the training paths, the best ratchet keeps the full minimum-variance hedge as its core ($\varphi=\RatchetFloor$), triggers at $z<\RatchetZ$ and decays with a half-life of \RatchetHl{} days. It runs \RatchetFc{} forecast-based sizing: in training the two variants were within noise of each other, and on the test paths the sized twin differs by \DEsFc{} pp (95\% CI [\DEsFcLo, \DEsFcHi]). The best switch enters at $z<\SwitchZ$ and exits at $z>\SwitchExit$.

\paragraph{Hedging performance.} Table~\ref{tab:hedge} summarises the test results, and Figure~\ref{fig:mech} shows the rules on one episode.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Always-on.} The minimum-variance hedge reduces ES by \EsRedAlways\% and variance by \VarRedAlways\% with a notional of only \NotionalAlways\% of the BTC position. A volatility index is a partial hedge for a long-BTC book: the simulated correlation between daily returns and \BVIV{} changes is \CorrDaily{} (\CorrDown{} on falling and \CorrUp{} on rising bars), and the variance reduction of a minimum-variance hedge is bounded by the squared correlation \citep{ederington1979hedging}.
  \item \emph{VWAP-switch.} The switch is the instructive failure. It trades \TurnSwitch{} times the BTC notional per year, and its ES reduction falls short of the always-on hedge by \DEsSwitch{} pp (95\% CI [\DEsSwitchLo, \DEsSwitchHi]). The gate fires on noise as often as on crashes, and each false alarm costs a round trip.
  \item \emph{VWAP-ratchet.} The ratchet reduces ES by \EsRedRatchet\% at \CostRatchet\% a year (always-on: \CostAlways\%). Paired across test paths, it beats the always-on hedge by \DEsRatchet{} pp (95\% CI [\DEsRatchetLo, \DEsRatchetHi]; higher on \ShareRatchet\% of paths) and offsets \DTailRatchet{} pp more of the tail losses, at the price of \DVarRatchet{} pp of variance reduction.
  \item \emph{Oracle.} Holding the hedge only in the latent stress regime reduces ES by just \EsRedOracle\%. Most simulated crash days start from calm, so regime timing without a core hedge leaves the book exposed.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Timing or size? A placebo test.} The ratchet holds more hedge on average than the always-on rule (\NotionalRatchet\% versus \NotionalAlways\% of notional), so its gain could come from size alone. To separate the two, we circularly shift each path's trigger series by 35 to 259 days, in multiples of a week. This keeps the number, clustering and hour-of-week profile of the triggers but breaks their link to the market. For the selected ratchet the placebo is just as good: it reduces ES by \EsRedPlacebo\%, and the ratchet beats it by only \DEsPlacebo{} pp (95\% CI [\DEsPlaceboLo, \DEsPlaceboHi]). Scaling the static hedge by 1.5 achieves the same ES reduction (\EsRedAlwaysOnehalf\% at \CostAlwaysOnehalf\%), although with less variance reduction (\VarRedAlwaysOnehalf\% versus \VarRedRatchet\%).

The grid tells a more nuanced story. Across all \PgridN{} ratchet configurations (\PgridPaths{} paths, \PgridShifts{} shifts each), the timing value averages \PgridMean{} pp, from \PgridMin{} to \PgridMax{} pp. \PgridSigPos{} configurations beat their placebo at the 5\% level and \PgridSigNeg{} lose to it. VWAP breakdowns therefore do carry information about tail days, but its value depends on the core hedge:
\begin{itemize}
  \item without a core ($\varphi=0$), timing adds \PgridFloorZero{} pp on average;
  \item with half a core ($\varphi=0.5$), it adds \PgridFloorHalf{} pp;
  \item with the full core ($\varphi=1$), it adds \PgridFloorOne{} pp.
\end{itemize}
Rules that rely on timing protect less (median ES reduction \PgridEsFloorZero\% without a core, against \PgridEsFloorOne\% with one), and the correlation between a configuration's timing value and its ES reduction is \PgridCorr. The most timing-dependent rule reaches \PgridBestEs\% at a cost of \PgridBestCost\% a year, against \PgridAlwaysEs\% for the always-on hedge on the same paths. Being hedged when the breakdown comes matters more than reacting to it.

\paragraph{Sizing.} Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} places every rule in cost-protection space. Along the scaled static hedge, ES reduction peaks between 1$\times$ and 1.5$\times$ and then falls (\EsRedAlwaysTwo\% at 2$\times$, \EsRedAlwaysThree\% at 3$\times$). The extra contracts add volatility-of-volatility risk faster than they add crash convexity. The ratchet configurations scatter around this frontier, and \ShareAboveLadder\% of them lie above it. The best of them do so through a better-shaped size profile, not timing: they buy the ES reduction of a 1.5$\times$ static hedge with more variance reduction and at lower cost. The economically first-order choice is how much \BVIV{} to hold.

\paragraph{Robustness.} Table~\ref{tab:robust} varies one assumption at a time.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Costs and carry.} Doubling or halving trading costs, or setting the carry to zero or twelve vol points, leaves the ratchet's edge over the always-on hedge at about +0.6 pp and significant.
  \item \emph{Spot-vol regime.} The edge holds under VIX-like dynamics (\RobVixLikeSpotVol{} pp) but shrinks to \RobInverseLeverage{} pp under inverse leverage (CI [\RobLoInverseLeverage, \RobHiInverseLeverage]). Here rallies lift \BVIV{}, and a hedge sized on falling bars covers less of what moves the index.
  \item \emph{Crash clustering.} When crashes cluster in the stress regime, the edge halves and loses significance (\RobCrashesClusterInStress{} pp, CI [\RobLoCrashesClusterInStress, \RobHiCrashesClusterInStress]). The larger post-breakdown position then also exposes the book to volatility crushes. On the hedged book's own worst days, the ratchet's hedge leg returns \DiagClusterRatchet\% of notional, against \DiagClusterAlways\% for the always-on hedge (baseline: \DiagBaseRatchet\% and \DiagBaseAlways\%). This echoes the equity lesson that badly timed protection can deepen losses \citep{israelov2019pathetic}.
  \item \emph{VWAP anchor and book.} A UTC-session anchor gives \RobSessionAnchoredVwap{} pp. Hedging a perpetual instead of spot gives \RobPerpetualBtcBook{} pp: BTC funding shifts the book's mean return but not its tail co-movement with \BVIV{}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation on live data}\label{sec:impl}

The pipeline (\texttt{scripts/run\_paper.py --live}) maps live data into the simulator's schema, so forecasting, signals, hedging and reporting share one code path. It uses the following sources:
\begin{itemize}
  \item BTCUSDT 15-minute klines from the Binance bulk archive, whose quote volume over volume gives each bar's exact VWAP, and BTC perpetual funding;
  \item index, mark and funding of the Bitfinex \BVIV{} perpetual since April 2024;
  \item \BVIV{} bars from the Volmex API, or DVOL as a proxy before 2024.
\end{itemize}
Four practical issues deserve attention. First, the \BVIV{} perpetuals are young and thin, so the costs in Table~\ref{tab:robust} should be stressed further for large books. Second, oracle risk is real on builder-deployed markets: in July 2026 a single erroneous pre-market print triggered large liquidations on another Hyperliquid HIP-3 perpetual. Third, venue mechanics differ: Bitfinex warns that its funding process can briefly pause trading, so a hedge should not depend on a single venue at the moment of a crash. Fourth, the 15-minute boundary itself attracts algorithmic order flow \citep{kim2026quarter}, so hedge orders should be worked inside the bar, for example by VWAP slicing \citep{berkowitz1988total,konishi2002optimal,bialkowski2008improving}, and not sent at the bar close \citep{madhavan2002vwap}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The evidence is simulation-based. The simulator links VWAP breakdowns to crashes only through regime drift and volatility clustering. Real liquidation cascades may make intraday breakdowns more informative, for instance through intraday momentum \citep{shen2022bitcoin}, or less. Our finding that a core hedge makes timing redundant is therefore a statement about this calibrated market, not a theorem. On live data, the placebo test of Section~\ref{sec:results} is the decisive check, and the pipeline runs it unchanged. The forecasting comparison is cleaner on real data too, because in the simulation the index is by construction a near-rational forecast; its ranking nevertheless matches the empirical literature \citep{hoang2020forecasting,bergsli2022forecasting}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\BVIV{} perpetuals turn BTC implied volatility into a hedging instrument, but a partial and costly one. Four practical lessons follow.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Forecast BTC volatility with HAR-type models at short horizons and with the implied index beyond a week; HAR augmented with implied variance is the robust default.
  \item Size a \BVIV{} hedge with the minimum-variance ratio, or up to about 1.5 times it if the aim is expected shortfall rather than variance, and keep it on.
  \item Do not switch the hedge around VWAP, because turnover eats the protection.
  \item Treat a 15-minute VWAP breakdown as information that is real but secondary. It helps a hedge that would otherwise be absent, but once a core hedge is held, a ratchet's gain is size, not timing.
\end{enumerate}
Any intraday timing rule should beat a placebo with the same trigger statistics before it earns its complexity.
